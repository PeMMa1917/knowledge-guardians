# Personas: game-wide critique

Each persona has four parts: **Values** (what they care about most), **Likes**, **Concerns** and **Solutions**. Notes point to missions, files or the numbers in [EVIDENCE.md](EVIDENCE.md). Fix numbers (`C-###`) are in [CONSENSUS.md](CONSENSUS.md). Disagreement numbers (`X-###`) are in [CONTESTED.md](CONTESTED.md). Chapter-level detail is in [chapters/](chapters/).

Short names used below: **standard loop** = compare two records, choose a display, a reader responds, revise. **Work words** = slip, strip, rail, peg, fold, runner, docket, tally (see EVIDENCE.md).

## A. Students and learners

### A1. Students (typical DP student, 16 to 18)
- **Values:** fun first; respect for their time; not being talked down to; a reason to care.
- **Likes:** the opening hook (three labels all claim "the first ballpoint pen", KG-CH1-M01) is concrete and odd. Showdowns feel like a fight they can win with evidence. Forging a billet and cutting it (KG-CH1-M02) is hands-on. Dov's Three Doors and Lanterns and Bones are real games. Customising Lumen and the quarters. Vesper's letters are personal.
- **Concerns:** after chapter 1 the play gets samey: the standard loop is 25 to 36 percent of missions in chapters 2, 3, 6, 7, 9, 11, 12 and 15, and there are almost no games or Showdowns after chapter 1 (EVIDENCE.md). The language is paperwork: "slip" appears 1,060 times. Many step titles do not say *why* ("Put the rests in use and keep the remaining request in view", KG-CH1-R13). Companions vanish into the background.
- **Solutions:** spread games and Showdowns to every chapter (C-004, C-006). Lead each step with the person who needs help and why (C-003). Let companions speak at key moments in every chapter (C-005).

### A2. High-achieving students
- **Values:** depth, challenge, being stretched, a credential-like sense of mastery, efficient use of time.
- **Likes:** real dated objects with MLA citations (825+ objects); the Skill Atlas and micro-skills; EE practice trails with the Title Lock; the Council ending that depends on reasons; constraint runs.
- **Concerns:** no visible "next level" of difficulty in later chapters; the same reasoning move (keep two readings apart) repeats; few chances to argue with named philosophers' ideas; the essay and exhibition skills are never rehearsed openly (X-002).
- **Solutions:** an optional "Hard reading" tier in Showdowns (fewer cards, stronger counterclaims) (C-004); Grand Fray superboss after chapter 15 (already planned in STATUS.md); named-idea keepsakes from real thinkers (X-015); optional "examiner's eye" journal view that names the TOK move after an encounter (C-009).

### A3. Low-achieving students
- **Values:** not feeling stupid; clear next step; quick wins; low reading load.
- **Likes:** hints never lower XP (rule 9); no game over (world rule 9); calm and zen modes; every reasoned judgement gets the same credit (OPEN-001), so there is no "wrong" that shames them.
- **Concerns:** objective titles are abstract and long (about 12 words per step; the hardest titles in EVIDENCE.md); dialogue is about 214,600 words; they cannot tell which of 94 chapter 1 missions matter. "Every choice is fine" can feel like "nothing I do matters" (X-008).
- **Solutions:** plain step titles with a place, an object and a verb (C-003); smart glossary for work words (C-002); a "what you changed" line after each choice so they see the effect (already in many encounters; check in C-003); main-story marker in the tracker (the tracker already shows one mission; add the main-story badge, C-010).

### A4. EAL students
- **Values:** clear English, words they can look up, time to read, no idioms without help, respect for their own languages.
- **Likes:** Simplified Technical English in many places; read-aloud with the browser voice; glossary with word histories; text scaling; local place names (Chomolungma and Sagarmatha).
- **Concerns:** work words have special meanings ("slip", "fold", "rail", "strip", "runner") that a dictionary does not give; many in-world nouns ("velin", "clamp tray", welcome.json); no language other than English; idioms in companion lines.
- **Solutions:** glossary entries for the most frequent work words (C-002); a plain-meaning note the first time an in-world word appears (C-003); a translation plan with human translators and a glossary of fixed terms (X-009).

### A5. Students with low intrinsic motivation
- **Values:** immediate payoff, humour, social reasons to play, short sessions, no "school".
- **Likes:** the rival Vesper, who is charming and sometimes right; pets and quarters; dice and cards; the Ballad that makes them a legend.
- **Concerns:** most rewards are marks and records; the first ten minutes are reading; side quests look like office tasks (sort slips, hang a proof); they cannot see progress on the map in later chapters.
- **Solutions:** a cold open with action in every chapter (story bible 9) (C-004); visible world change after each chapter (lamps, repaired bridges) (C-011); more comedy encounters (The Goat Who Was Certain is planned) (C-004).

### A6. Students with high intrinsic motivation
- **Values:** curiosity, freedom to explore, secrets, depth on demand.
- **Likes:** iceberg depth (Lore codex, Undercroft index, letters, encyclopedia lookups); 1,050 keepsakes; Blue Tail mystery across 15 chapters; Harrowmere clues.
- **Concerns:** depth is broad but flat: many objects give the same kind of check; few open-ended investigations where they set the question.
- **Solutions:** a "Noisy Board" mystery with many irrelevant and conflicting clues (adapted from the History Mystery lesson) where the player decides what counts as evidence (C-004, DRIVE_WEAVE.md); Question Stones that open optional rooms (planned).

### A7. Students who love TOK
- **Values:** big questions, debate, being right about subtle things, recognising TOK ideas.
- **Likes:** knowledge questions on every mission; the Fray as a visible cost of over-claiming; Showdowns with concessions and reframes; real cases (Cottingley, Gombe, Turin Strike Papyrus).
- **Concerns:** the big ideas are under the surface; ethics frameworks, paradigm shifts, falsification and testimony are rarely named; they want to argue more often than once or twice.
- **Solutions:** more Showdowns (C-006); optional "TOK words for what you did" in the journal (C-009); lens cards in a History on Trial style debate (DRIVE_WEAVE.md).

### A8. Students who do not like TOK
- **Values:** relevance to their subjects or life, no jargon, no "what is knowledge?" lectures.
- **Likes:** the game rarely uses TOK jargon in play; concrete jobs (deliver, repair, forge, weigh); DP subject links (chemistry in the forge, maths in the fair games).
- **Concerns:** knowledge questions in the tracker look like homework prompts; if C-009 adds TOK words, the game could start to feel like class.
- **Solutions:** keep TOK words out of play and in the journal only (C-009 condition); let subject links show in the world (a biology field test, an economics market) rather than in labels.

### A9. Student learners from a variety of learning perspectives
- **Values:** more than one way in: reading, listening, doing, drawing, talking, building.
- **Likes:** read-aloud; hands-on benches; music box and bell stair; drawing in Star Pictures.
- **Concerns:** most encounters are read-then-choose; few let the player draw, build, sequence or perform; sound-based tasks need the caption fallback.
- **Solutions:** vary input types per chapter: drag-to-sequence, draw, rhythm, physical simulation (C-004). Keep the claim from the learning-styles myth out (see H11).

## B. Schools, families and the IB

### B1. Other TOK teachers
- **Values:** alignment with the course, usable in a 45 to 80 minute lesson, material they can discuss afterwards, low prep.
- **Likes:** the teacher view (formative, class totals, misconception alerts that say they may be wrong); knowledge questions per mission; real objects with sources; the exhibition-like Alcove of Firsts.
- **Concerns:** they cannot see quickly which encounter teaches which concept or theme; no lesson-sized entry points ("play the Cold Store in lesson 4"); chapter 1 has 94 missions, so a class scatters.
- **Solutions:** a teacher map generated from weave tags (C-008); chapter starts from a teacher-chosen save (exists for chapter 2; extend); good stopping points (C-010).

### B2. Head of TOK department
- **Values:** consistency across teachers, coverage of the guide, evidence of impact, defensible choices for leadership.
- **Likes:** coverage audits (TOK floor, micro-skills, 30-minute windows); privacy-first design.
- **Concerns:** no simple coverage summary for a department meeting; no guidance on sequencing with the course.
- **Solutions:** C-008 teacher map with a one-page summary per theme and area of knowledge.

### B3. Other subject teachers
- **Values:** their subject shown correctly; links they can use; no distortion of method.
- **Likes:** subject weave tags (biology, chemistry, physics, economics, history, maths, visual arts, theatre); real instruments and specimens.
- **Concerns:** subject links are mostly tags, not play; some subjects have only optional encounters (Language ab initio, world religions, sports science, docs/PLAYTEST_PRIORITIES.md); a science teacher may object to "simulation" results presented near history.
- **Solutions:** at least one main-route encounter per subject group (C-012); subject-expert review of each subject's encounters (process, CONSENSUS.md).

### B4. Classroom teachers (international, national and local curricula)
- **Values:** works on any school device; works without IB background; no install; classroom management.
- **Likes:** browser-only; offline-capable static site; keyboard play.
- **Concerns:** the game assumes IB terms in the teacher view; school filters may block encyclopedia links; there is no "pause the class" or classroom-mode.
- **Solutions:** teacher guide with non-IB wording (C-008); links already open only on click (no change); a simple shared "chapter goal" card the teacher can project (C-010).

### B5. IB Diploma Programme coordinator
- **Values:** alignment with DP policies (academic integrity, assessment rules, CAS and EE boundaries), workload, data protection.
- **Likes:** EE and CAS appear as practice only; the game never asks for a student's EE question (EE-002); play is never CAS.
- **Concerns:** the IB may see the game as unofficial assessment practice; students might copy game wording into IAs.
- **Solutions:** a short statement in the teacher guide: the game is not IB-endorsed, does not mark, and its wording must not be used in assessed work (C-007).

### B6. School principal or head of school (high-performing school)
- **Values:** reputation, results, parent confidence, innovation that is safe.
- **Likes:** depth, real sources, privacy, accessibility.
- **Concerns:** can it raise essay and exhibition results? Is there evidence? Will parents see it as "games instead of study"?
- **Solutions:** a one-page parent and leadership summary with the learning design (C-007); a playtest study plan (docs/PLAYTEST_PRIORITIES.md) with transfer tasks.

### B7. School principal or head of school (low-performing school)
- **Values:** engagement, attendance, low cost, old devices, staff with little TOK training.
- **Likes:** free, browser-based, no accounts needed (local mode), low-end friendly pixel art.
- **Concerns:** reading load and abstract steps lose weaker readers; 2.1 MB bundle and 112 maps on old Chromebooks; teachers need a script.
- **Solutions:** C-002, C-003, C-010; a low-end performance check on school hardware (QA, C-013).

### B8. Parents and guardians
- **Values:** safety, no hidden spending, no strangers, appropriate content, screen time with purpose.
- **Likes:** no chat, no user-made content, no ads, no purchases, no news; friendships not romance; no real religion as an enemy.
- **Concerns:** what data is kept; whether the game is "study"; difficult history (the Harrowmere flood is fictional, but real objects include human remains such as the Chinchorro mummies).
- **Solutions:** a plain privacy and content note for families (C-007); content notes before sensitive real objects (C-014).

### B9. IT specialists
- **Values:** easy deployment, no installs, works on managed devices, offline, predictable storage, no surprise network calls.
- **Likes:** static Vite build; no server; no third-party scripts; local fonts (Lexend from fontsource); assets checked by `npm run assets:check`.
- **Concerns:** saves live in one browser profile; shared or wiped devices lose progress; localStorage quota on managed Chromebooks; no documented list of hosts and storage keys.
- **Solutions:** an "IT at a glance" page: storage keys, size, how to clear, what the export file holds, no network calls, how to host (C-007); the accounts plan (Cloudflare, username-only) is decided (docs/ACCOUNTS_AND_HOSTING.md on PR #7).

### B10. Data security professionals
- **Values:** least data, no personal data off the device, clear retention, threat model, children's data law (GDPR, COPPA, FERPA and local laws).
- **Likes:** no network; teacher files are shared by student choice; score files carry no name; portfolio private by default; misconception alerts with "Not a misconception".
- **Concerns:** the progress file trusts the player name (two students with the same name merge, STATUS.md); exported files can travel by email; future accounts raise new risks.
- **Solutions:** document a data-flow diagram and retention in C-007; recommend pseudonymous names in class; keep the accounts design blind to save contents (already in the accounts plan).

### B11. The IB itself
- **Values:** fidelity to the TOK guide and its intent; no misuse of IB marks, wording or assessment instruments; academic integrity; international-mindedness.
- **Likes:** play-first approach that trains habits (evidence, perspective, responsibility) instead of answers; the knowledge framework (scope, perspectives, methods and tools, ethics) tagged everywhere; no IB wording in the public repository (AGENTS.md rule 2).
- **Concerns:** any hint of official endorsement; use of past prescribed titles or exhibition prompts; reproduced subject report wording.
- **Solutions:** keep IB documents private (they are in Mika's private folder, not the repository); a disclaimer in the credits and teacher guide (C-007); never quote prompts or titles in the game (X-002 keeps this boundary).

### B12. TOK course designers and curriculum reviewers
- **Values:** balance across the core theme, optional themes and areas of knowledge; the 12 concepts; progression.
- **Likes:** the 30-minute opportunity floor audit; DOK, Bloom, Fink and SOLO tags; five campaign models.
- **Concerns:** optional themes (knowledge and technology, language, politics, religion, indigenous societies) are uneven; ethics as a framework part is thinner than methods; arts as an area of knowledge is thin outside the House of Performance.
- **Solutions:** coverage by theme in the teacher map (C-008); new encounters chosen to fill the thinnest themes first (C-004 plan per chapter).

### B13. Curriculum designers
- **Values:** coherent sequence, prerequisite knowledge, spiralling, clear outcomes.
- **Likes:** methods return in new places (the Date-Kind Lens in several worlds; the fair test).
- **Concerns:** chapters do not build in difficulty in a visible way; the same move recurs without a raised demand.
- **Solutions:** a progression table per chapter: what is new, what returns, what is harder (add to each chapter audit) (C-015).

### B14. Universities
- **Values:** students who reason with evidence, cite sources, handle uncertainty, write clearly.
- **Likes:** MLA citations of earned objects; distinguishing original, copy and reconstruction; qualifiers kept ("probably").
- **Concerns:** little practice with extended argument; no explicit link to research ethics (consent, anonymity) except in a few encounters.
- **Solutions:** research-ethics moments in the Archive of Voices and the Living Forest (C-004 chapter plans); EE practice trails extended to two more worlds.

### B15. UNESCO experts
- **Values:** education for sustainable development, global citizenship, cultural diversity, open access, inclusion.
- **Likes:** many peoples treated separately; water, forest and drought chapters; free licences; offline use.
- **Concerns:** English only; no statement of open educational resource licence (code licence undecided, STATUS.md); global South devices.
- **Solutions:** choose a licence (Mika decision, X-016); translation plan (X-009); low-end performance (C-013).

## C. Assessment

### C1. Head TOK examiner and senior examiner team
- **Values:** valid reasoning, knowledge questions that are really about knowledge, specific examples, balanced evaluation, implications.
- **Likes:** the label builder (force matches support) trains the claim–evidence match that examiners reward; concessions gain trust; real specific objects are good exhibition practice.
- **Concerns:** players may not transfer moves to writing; "keep both readings" can become a formula (a both-sides habit without a judgement).
- **Solutions:** encounters that ask the player to *commit* to a weighted judgement with a stated reason, while still giving equal credit for any reasoned answer (C-016); optional journal "examiner's eye" (C-009).

### C2. TOK examiners who mark essays and exhibitions
- **Values:** clear link between object and prompt; justification; avoiding descriptive answers.
- **Likes:** the Alcove of Firsts ("stand a real object, say what knowledge it shows, answer the So-What Sphinx", KG-CH1-X01) is close to the exhibition skill.
- **Concerns:** it appears once, early, and is optional.
- **Solutions:** a returning exhibition-style alcove in three later worlds (C-004), still a game (visitors react), never a graded task.

### C3. Assessment experts
- **Values:** validity, reliability, fairness, formative feedback, avoiding perverse incentives.
- **Likes:** XP never reduced by hints; fixed maxima; misconception thresholds with uncertainty.
- **Concerns:** counts of practice are not evidence of learning (docs/PLAYTEST_PRIORITIES.md says so); the leaderboard measures activity, which can reward grinding.
- **Solutions:** keep the leaderboard optional and activity-only (X-001); add transfer tasks for playtests (an unfamiliar object) in the teacher guide (C-008).

## D. Philosophy and the academy

### D1. Philosophy teachers
- **Values:** precise concepts, named arguments, good counterexamples, ethics with frameworks.
- **Likes:** the fair test and Monty Hall-style doors; the Gettier-like gap between a true label and a justified one is implicit in many encounters.
- **Concerns:** ethics frameworks (consequences, duties, virtues) are not named or contrasted; classic cases (Gettier, the bat, the extended mind) are absent.
- **Solutions:** an ethics encounter where townspeople reason from different frameworks about one dilemma, adapted from the ethics lessons (DRIVE_WEAVE.md) (C-004); philosophers as keepsake givers (X-015).

### D2. Philosophers
- **Values:** intellectual honesty, no false neutrality, distinction between relativism and pluralism.
- **Likes:** "no hidden right answer" applies only to contested matters; documented claims still need sources; the game distinguishes claim kinds from historical labels (rule 8).
- **Concerns:** OPEN-001 can slide into "every view is equally good". Some choices are not contested and should have consequences in truth, not only in story.
- **Solutions:** in each chapter, at least one encounter where a claim is plainly false on the evidence and the world shows it (the Fray already does this for over-claiming; make it visible in later chapters) (C-016). Philosophers and designers disagree on how strong this should be (X-008).

### D3. The wider TOK and philosophy education community
- **Values:** shared resources, credit for sources, openness.
- **Likes:** public repository, credits, sources.
- **Concerns:** the game adapts many lesson ideas; credit to lesson authors is unclear.
- **Solutions:** DRIVE_WEAVE.md lists adapted ideas (not wording) with the kind of source; ask Mika how to credit lesson authors (X-017).

### D4. Education researchers and academics
- **Values:** testable claims, measures, ethics approval.
- **Likes:** route models, learning audit, reproducible tools.
- **Concerns:** no study design; teacher-view data is formative and minimal, which limits research.
- **Solutions:** a separate, consented research build is a Mika decision (X-018); keep the classroom build minimal.

### D5. Game design researchers and academics
- **Values:** clear design rationale, ludonarrative fit, player-type theory used with care.
- **Likes:** the Fray as a mechanic that carries the theme (story bible 3); fair randomness rules.
- **Concerns:** the standard loop is a "procedural rhetoric" that may teach "always keep both" rather than "judge well".
- **Solutions:** C-016; vary verbs (C-004).

### D6. Game-based learning researchers
- **Values:** intrinsic integration (learning *is* the play), transfer, debriefing.
- **Likes:** intrinsic integration is strong in the label builder, Showdowns and field games.
- **Concerns:** weaker in specimen-room handovers (the learning is in reading text, not in the mechanic).
- **Solutions:** turn the busiest specimen-room tasks into manipulations (rotate, align, weigh) where the TOK idea is in the action (C-004).

### D7. Historians
- **Values:** sources, context, provenance, avoiding presentism, care with the dead and with trauma.
- **Likes:** documented vs reconstruction vs imagined labels; MLA citations; museum catalogue links; human remains handled as records with context.
- **Concerns:** treaty and battle (Qadesh) handled well, but historiography (how historians disagree, schools of thought) is little shown; the History on Trial lesson idea is absent.
- **Solutions:** lens cards debate in a later world (C-004, DRIVE_WEAVE.md); difficult real history only with care and Mika's approval (X-005).

### D8. Researchers on engaged citizenship
- **Values:** public reasoning, responsibility to communities, civic action.
- **Likes:** many encounters are about who receives a correction and who bears the cost; the Civic Machine dispatch rule; the Water Commons drought plan.
- **Concerns:** the player acts for people but rarely *with* them; communities rarely push back.
- **Solutions:** a town meeting Showdown where residents bring their own claims (C-006).

## E. Game makers

### E1. Game designers
- **Values:** core loop clarity, variety, pacing, meaningful choice, feedback.
- **Likes:** distinct core verbs in chapter 1 (lens, test, label, Showdown, thread, forge); fail-forward; Waystones.
- **Concerns:** after chapter 1 the verbs shrink to read, choose, place (EVIDENCE.md: "Argue" 0 to 1 per chapter, "Play" 0 to 4); 94 missions in chapter 1 is a front-loaded design; consequences are often a text line, not a change in later play.
- **Solutions:** one signature mechanic per chapter (C-004); Showdowns in four more chapters (C-006); Echoes that change the map (story bible gap 9) (C-011).

### E2. Writers and narrative designers
- **Values:** voice, want and need, subtext, scene goals, conflict.
- **Likes:** story bible (central dramatic question, Vesper who is sometimes right, Harrowmere mystery); Hearth talks; loyalty missions.
- **Concerns:** the busiest speakers are specimen-room keepers who speak in instructions (EVIDENCE.md: Aven 293 scenes); core cast speaks little in dialogue scenes; most lines describe procedures, not people.
- **Solutions:** a voice sheet for every speaker with more than 20 scenes and a test that checks it (C-005); rewrite the first and last line of each busy keeper so the person shows (C-005).

### E3. Game writers and narrative designers (branching, barks, systemic text)
- **Values:** reactivity, barks, branch economy.
- **Likes:** 64 Echo lines; world-memory lines; letters that read flags.
- **Concerns:** many branches end in near-identical text ("Both routes leave a visible result"); barks are rare in later worlds.
- **Solutions:** each branch should change a person's attitude or a later option (story bible 10); add barks per chapter (C-011).

### E4. Teen fiction writers
- **Values:** emotional truth, identity, friendship, stakes for a young protagonist, humour.
- **Likes:** the player as a young Guardian who must admit mistakes; Kip's family story; Rook's guilt.
- **Concerns:** few scenes where the player's own feelings matter; no peer of the player's age in the cast; romance is excluded (X-004).
- **Solutions:** a peer character (an apprentice Guardian) who grows across chapters (C-017, plan); friendships with real conflict and repair.

### E5. New adult genre authors
- **Values:** independence, first adult responsibilities, moral grey zones.
- **Likes:** Ise's institutional cover-up and the choice to expose it; Vesper's sincere case.
- **Concerns:** the stakes are mostly about labels and displays, not about the player's own future.
- **Solutions:** the player's role (Knower role) should open and close real options in later chapters (C-011).

### E6. Artists
- **Values:** one cohesive style, readable silhouettes, colour scripts per chapter, lighting.
- **Likes:** rich pixel art with lighting; procedural illustrations in `src/ui/illustrations.ts`; chapter colour accents.
- **Concerns:** many specimen panels are text-heavy with schematic art; 159 characters need distinct silhouettes; some later maps reuse tiles.
- **Solutions:** a colour script per chapter in the story bible (C-018); prioritise art for the encounters with the most traffic.

### E7. Game programmers and artists (technical art)
- **Values:** performance, atlas reuse, lighting budget.
- **Likes:** whole-number zoom; reduced-effects mode.
- **Concerns:** `src/ui/ui.ts` is 5,677 lines; each new encounter adds a branch in `openFieldGame`; risk of regressions.
- **Solutions:** a registry for field games (id to panel) so new games do not grow `ui.ts` (C-019).

### E8. Programmers and engineers
- **Values:** maintainability, tests, clear boundaries.
- **Likes:** domain layer without Phaser; 2,263 tests; strict TypeScript; save migrations.
- **Concerns:** the content pack is large (6.5 MB of content JSON); many near-duplicate TypeScript content generators; test suite takes about 2 minutes.
- **Solutions:** C-019; content linting for repeated templates (the audit tool helps).

### E9. Composers and sound designers
- **Values:** leitmotifs, adaptive music, sound that fits the task.
- **Likes:** one synthesised score per world (`src/domain/campaignScore.ts`); the music box motif; captions for sounds.
- **Concerns:** only three recorded loops; tasks do not change the music (thinking vs discovery vs tension); the Vesper harpsichord variant and the three music box versions need checking in play.
- **Solutions:** task layers (calm pad for reading, bright sting for discovery) using the existing synth voices (C-020).

### E10. QA testers and playtesters
- **Values:** reproducible bugs, clear builds, coverage of branches.
- **Likes:** route tests; browser checks log.
- **Concerns:** many staged prerequisites in browser checks; phone layout untested for several panels (STATUS.md).
- **Solutions:** a phone pass for the label builder, forge and Title Lock (C-013); keep a playtest script per chapter.

### E11. AAA studio developer
- **Values:** production pipelines, polish, scope control, vertical slice.
- **Likes:** strong pillars (story bible), content data separate from code.
- **Concerns:** scope is very wide for the team size; polish is uneven; the first hour must be the most polished.
- **Solutions:** treat chapter 1's main route as the vertical slice (C-021 polish list in CH01.md).

### E12. Indie developers
- **Values:** a sharp hook, personality, small scope done well.
- **Likes:** the Fray idea; Lumen who can be wrong; the mirror scene at the end.
- **Concerns:** the hook is buried under side content; the game's personality is strongest in chapter 1.
- **Solutions:** carry chapter 1's humour and odd specifics into later chapters (C-005).

### E13. Serious games designers
- **Values:** clear learning objectives, debrief, stakeholder fit.
- **Likes:** teacher view, portfolio, reflection after a signal.
- **Concerns:** debriefs are mostly text in the journal; teachers have no debrief prompts.
- **Solutions:** debrief prompts per chapter in the teacher map (C-008).

### E14. Computer science teachers
- **Values:** systems, algorithms, ethics of technology, code quality as a teaching example.
- **Likes:** the Brass Sorter (a machine that learns from examples); the Civic Machine dispatch rule; open code.
- **Concerns:** knowledge and technology is thin outside chapters 1 and 12.
- **Solutions:** a machine-bias encounter in the Civic Machine using the ethics-of-the-algorithm lesson idea (DRIVE_WEAVE.md).

### E15. Game design teachers
- **Values:** a game students can study; documented design decisions.
- **Likes:** STORY_BIBLE, GAME_ELEMENTS and STORY_ELEMENTS docs; design rules in AGENTS.md.
- **Concerns:** no post-mortem style notes.
- **Solutions:** this audit and CONTESTED.md serve as design-decision records.

## F. Critics, press and community

### F1. Game journalists
- **Values:** a story angle, novelty, screenshots.
- **Likes:** "an RPG where you fight misinformation by writing honest labels"; the rival who is sometimes right.
- **Concerns:** hard to explain the standard loop in a screenshot.
- **Solutions:** signature mechanics per chapter (C-004) give each chapter a visual hook.

### F2. Game critics
- **Values:** coherence, pacing, respect for the player, originality.
- **Likes:** ludonarrative harmony of the Fray; no game over.
- **Concerns:** repetition and length; the procedural dialogue voice; consequences mostly textual.
- **Solutions:** C-004, C-005, C-011.

### F3. EdTech reviewers and education journalists
- **Values:** learning evidence, privacy, teacher usability, cost.
- **Likes:** free, private, offline, no ads.
- **Concerns:** no published learning evidence; onboarding for teachers.
- **Solutions:** C-007, C-008; a playtest report when available.

### F4. Community managers and marketing team

## H. Learning sciences and pedagogy

### H1. Pedagogists and education theorists
- **Values:** constructivism, dialogue, agency, reflection.
- **Likes:** consequence-based learning; reflection prompts; no hidden right answer.
- **Concerns:** little dialogue between the player and peers; reflection is optional and textual.

### H2. Learning scientists
- **Values:** retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, cognitive load.
- **Likes:** methods return in new worlds (spacing and interleaving); Lumen's four depths act as faded worked examples.
- **Concerns:** cognitive load in dialogue is high (many new nouns per scene); retrieval is implicit.
- **Solutions:** C-002, C-003; Echo moments that ask the player to *use* an earlier method without a reminder (C-011).

### H3. Cognition researchers
- **Values:** attention, working memory, transfer, misconceptions.
- **Likes:** misconception signals with fixed rules and thresholds; intuition-first games (doors, birthday paradox) that use surprise.
- **Concerns:** many near-identical choices may cause habituation.
- **Solutions:** C-004.

### H4. Researchers on engagement
- **Values:** behavioural, emotional, cognitive and agentic engagement.
- **Likes:** agency in endings; humour in chapter 1.
- **Concerns:** emotional engagement drops after chapter 2.

### H5. Gamification experts

### H6. Gamified learning experts

## J. Culture, language and community

### J1. People from different Indigenous communities
*These notes use published principles. They are not testimony. Real advisors from each people must review any content about them.*
- **Values (from published principles):** collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility and ethics (CARE); free, prior and informed consent; protocols for sacred and restricted knowledge; living cultures, not past ones.
- **Likes:** sacred things and medicines are never items (spec CARE rules); 72 peoples are locked until advisors approve; Story Walks have OPEN, LIVING, RESTRICT and MYTH-ABOUT labels.
- **Concerns:** oral history must not be treated as weaker evidence by default; game mechanics that "check" oral accounts against written ones can repeat a colonial hierarchy; the lesson on two accounts of one encounter (DRIVE_WEAVE.md) needs the community's own framing.
- **Solutions:** keep the advisor gate (X-006); design oral-history encounters where the oral account checks the written one too (C-024).

### J2. Cultural critics
- **Values:** power in representation, who tells whose story, gaze.
- **Likes:** "who gets remembered" is already a theme (Hall of Firsts, contributor credit in Gombe records).
- **Concerns:** a fictional institution (the Nexus) curates real peoples' objects, which mirrors museum power; the game should name that.
- **Solutions:** the Harrowmere arc already questions the Nexus; add one object whose custodians ask for it to be returned or shown differently (with care) (C-023).

### J3. Linguists
- **Values:** accurate claims about language, no linguistic determinism myths, respect for language variety.
- **Likes:** the Missing Word (connotation and denotation); the ferry cant; translation puzzles.
- **Concerns:** the strong Sapir–Whorf idea should not be presented as fact; the game's own register is narrow (paperwork English).
- **Solutions:** a language encounter that contrasts weak and strong linguistic relativity with real evidence (C-004); vary registers by place and person (C-005).
